Walking the old canal road

Beyond the station

The towpath narrowed after the third lock, where willow roots had lifted the gravel. I slowed down and watched for the faded distance markers.

The station noise disappeared within half an hour. I could hear water pass through cracks in the first gate and the dry click of seed heads against the fence. A cyclist stopped to compare the modern trail map with a hand-drawn route copied from an old canal guide.

Beyond the lock keeper's cottage, the road crossed from the shaded bank to an exposed shelf of limestone. The canal company had planted poplars there in a straight line. Most were gone, but their stumps still marked the measured pace of the original road.

The abandoned basin

At noon I reached a turning basin that looked like an ordinary pond. A low stone edge revealed its older purpose. Cargo boats once waited there while crews checked the next lock, and a narrow ramp let horses reach water without leaving the towpath.

I ate beside the ramp and watched insects settle on the surface. The place felt remote, but rooflines were visible through the trees. This tension followed the whole route: every quiet view was also a piece of infrastructure placed close to farms, workshops, and homes.

The final crossing

I reached the stone aqueduct before dusk. Water moved through the valley below while the canal stayed almost still.

The aqueduct parapet was too high to see over from the path, so I climbed a service stair on the valley side. From that landing, the canal seemed to end at the sky. Only the worn strip along the towpath showed that people still crossed it every day.

I had expected the walk to feel like a search for remains. Instead, it showed how an obsolete transport system can keep organizing a place. Roads follow its embankments, gardens use its water, and villages turn old warehouses toward the trail. The canal is no longer separate from the landscape. It has become one of the lines that holds the landscape together.
